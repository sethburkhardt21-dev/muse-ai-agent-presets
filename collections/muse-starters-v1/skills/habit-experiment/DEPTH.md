# Try a Small Habit Change and Review What Happens — operational depth

Read only the branch needed for the current task. These are working methods, not a demand for additional approvals on ordinary reading or drafting.

## Domain method

Treat a habit change as a small, reversible experiment with a defined opportunity, behavior and observation period. Clarify what counts as an opportunity and what counts as completion before reviewing outcomes. Keep the person’s purpose and burden in view; tracking should serve the goal rather than become the goal.

Preserve missing observations as unknown, not automatic success or failure. Compare similar contexts and distinguish adherence from the broader outcome the person cares about. A before-and-after change can be useful personal evidence without establishing causation. Change one major variable at a time when that makes the next observation more informative.

## Output contract

Deliver a bounded experiment plan or review with opportunity count, observed completions, missing records, context changes and a proportionate next adjustment. Avoid diagnostic, medical or guaranteed-effect claims. Do not create reminders or monitoring jobs unless actually authorized and executed.

## Choose the right action

### The desired change is vague.
Define a small observable behavior and an opportunity that fits the person’s routine.

**Avoid:** Do not begin with an elaborate tracking system.

### The tracking record has gaps.
Show observed and missing opportunities separately.

**Avoid:** Do not silently count missing entries as failures or successes.

### The behavior is completed but the desired outcome does not improve.
Separate adherence from outcome and inspect timing and context.

**Avoid:** Do not call the intervention successful solely because the checkbox was marked.

### Several conditions changed at once.
Record the confounding changes and narrow the next experiment.

**Avoid:** Do not attribute the entire difference to one habit.

### The burden or downside outweighs the value.
Reduce, pause or stop the experiment using the person’s priorities.

**Avoid:** Do not insist on streak preservation at the expense of wellbeing.

## Worked example (authored, synthetic)

**Input:** Three opportunities are recorded: one complete, one incomplete and one unknown.

**Method:** Compute the observed rate using the two known observations while retaining all three opportunities.

**Result:** One success out of two observed opportunities; one record is missing. This is not proof of a causal effect.

## Edge or recovery example 1 (authored, synthetic)

**Input:** The missing observation is later confirmed complete.

**Method:** Update the original opportunity rather than adding another duplicate record.

**Result:** Two successes out of three observed opportunities. The changed rate reflects new evidence, not a rewritten denominator.

## Failure diagnosis

### The success rate changes depending on omitted days.
**Likely cause:** The denominator was selected after seeing results.

**Repair:** Use the predefined opportunity set and show missingness.

**Recheck:** The rate and its coverage are transparent.

### A short improvement is described as a proven effect.
**Likely cause:** Observation and causal inference were conflated.

**Repair:** State the observed change and relevant alternatives.

**Recheck:** The conclusion does not exceed the design.

### The person abandons the plan because tracking is exhausting.
**Likely cause:** Measurement burden was ignored.

**Repair:** Simplify the record and choose a smaller useful question.

**Recheck:** The experiment remains practical and voluntary.
