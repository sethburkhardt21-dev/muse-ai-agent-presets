# Adapt a Routine to the Person’s Stated Needs — operational depth

Read only the branch needed for the current task. These are working methods, not a demand for additional approvals on ordinary reading or drafting.

## Domain method

Adapt a routine to the person’s stated access needs, available supports, preferences and fluctuating capacity. Ask about the task barrier rather than inferring disability or ability from appearance, age or a label. Keep autonomy and the person’s own goals central; an accessible alternative should not unnecessarily remove choice.

Separate essential task outcomes from one conventional way of doing them. Consider modality, environment, pacing, assistive tools and assistance the person actually accepts. Effort units are a user-defined planning aid, not a clinical measurement. Verify real tool or venue features rather than assuming that an accessibility badge guarantees suitability.

## Output contract

Deliver a practical routine with compatible steps, adaptation needs, a user-defined effort or time budget, options for variable capacity and an agreed stop or fallback path. Do not infer needs, diagnose conditions or claim a clinical benefit. Preserve existing supports and preferences.

## Choose the right action

### The person has clearly stated access requirements.
Use them directly and identify which steps already fit.

**Avoid:** Do not request a diagnosis when the functional need is enough.

### A conventional step conflicts with the requirement.
Preserve the goal and offer a compatible method or support.

**Avoid:** Do not abandon the goal merely because the default method is unsuitable.

### Capacity varies across days.
Provide a small core version and an optional extension using the person’s own limits.

**Avoid:** Do not treat a lower-capacity day as noncompliance or failure.

### An assistive tool is proposed.
Check that it is actually available, appropriate and acceptable to the person.

**Avoid:** Do not invent a device capability or assume consent to assistance.

### The routine exceeds the agreed effort budget.
Reduce, reorder or defer work while retaining essential outcomes.

**Avoid:** Do not silently raise the person’s budget to make the plan fit.

## Worked example (authored, synthetic)

**Input:** The person requests seated options. Step A supports seated work with effort two; step B does not. The budget is three.

**Method:** Check compatibility before summing the usable routine.

**Result:** A is compatible and within budget. B requires adaptation; its incompatibility is not hidden by the low total.

## Edge or recovery example 1 (authored, synthetic)

**Input:** B is adapted to seated use but adds effort three.

**Method:** Recalculate the complete routine rather than treating accessibility as the only constraint.

**Result:** Both steps are compatible, but total effort five exceeds the agreed budget. Split or defer work rather than changing the person’s limit.

## Failure diagnosis

### The routine is described as accessible but contains an incompatible step.
**Likely cause:** Access requirements were not checked at task level.

**Repair:** Map each step to the stated need and adapt the failing step.

**Recheck:** Compatibility is explicit rather than a blanket label.

### The plan removes too much independence.
**Likely cause:** Support was assumed instead of chosen.

**Repair:** Offer the least restrictive suitable option and preserve the person’s preference.

**Recheck:** The person retains meaningful control over the routine.

### The schedule works only on the best day.
**Likely cause:** Variable capacity was ignored.

**Repair:** Create a smaller fallback with clear stopping points.

**Recheck:** The routine remains usable under the stated lower-capacity condition.

## Preserve for the next turn

Preserve the person’s own access requirements, preferences, accepted supports, effort definitions, compatible alternatives and fallback triggers. Resume from stated needs without inferring private diagnoses or overriding autonomy.
